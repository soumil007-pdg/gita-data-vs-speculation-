The three-layer logic
Layer 1 — Find a speech verb in the opening speech_verbs = ['speak', 'explain', 'ask', 'say', 'tell', 'conclude', 'declare', 'reply', 'narrate', ...] Layer 2 — Find its grammatical subject (nsubj) if token.dep_ == "nsubj" and token.head == speech_verb: detected = token.text Layer 3 — Carry forward if nothing found if detected: last_speaker = detected else: speakers.append(last_speaker) # same speaker continues

◐ Why not just use an ML model?
We considered a Hugging Face speaker attribution model — but models trained on modern English news misread archaic Gita language ("The Blessed Lord spoke unto Arjuna"). Rules + dependency parsing gave us ~90% accuracy with zero false positives on clear markers. ML was only considered as a fallback for the hard 10%.

Section 5 · Deep NLP

The Psychological Map — All 700 Verses

We ran every verse through SamLowe/roberta-base-go_emotions — a RoBERTa model fine-tuned on 58,000 Reddit comments across 28 emotion categories. The result is an emotion fingerprint for every verse in the Gita.

Why GoEmotions over a simpler model
Model: j-hartmann/emotion-english-distilroberta-base Emotions: joy, sadness, anger, fear, surprise, disgust, neutral Problem for the Gita: "The soul cannot be cut by weapons" → classified as neutral "I bow before Your infinite greatness" → classified as joy These are philosophically admiration and realization — not neutral or simple joy. 7 buckets are too crude.
Model: SamLowe/roberta-base-go_emotions ← what we used Emotions: 28 categories including admiration, realization, curiosity, caring, approval, awe, relief, pride... "The soul cannot be cut by weapons" → realization (0.52) "I bow before Your infinite greatness" → admiration (0.71) These map to the philosophical texture of the Gita. 28 emotions gives you nuance. 7 gives you noise.
How the pipeline works
# One call to classify all 700 verses at once verse_list = a_english['Enlgish Translation'].astype(str).tolist() raw_results = emotion_classifier(verse_list) # Each verse returns 28 dictionaries — one per emotion raw_results[0][0] # → [{'label': 'admiration', 'score': 0.51}, ...] # top_k=None means we get ALL 28 emotions, not just the winner # This lets us track subtle emotions that aren't #1 but still present

Section 6 · Emotional Arc

Krishna's Emotional Journey Across 18 Chapters

564 verses. 18 chapters. We averaged all 28 emotion scores per chapter to find where Krishna's teaching mood shifts — from approval to caring to love at the cosmic vision.

What the heatmap reveals — key chapters
approval peaks at Ch5 (0.21) and Ch17 (0.22) Ch5 = Karma-Sannyasa Yoga — "both paths lead to liberation" Krishna approves of those who renounce selfish desire Ch17 = Three kinds of faith — "faith shapes the person" Krishna approves of those who worship with pure intent Pattern: approval rises whenever Krishna validates a path
caring peaks at Ch11 (0.18) — the Vishwaroop chapter Arjuna has just seen the terrifying cosmic form. He is overwhelmed, trembling, begging for mercy. Krishna's caring spikes — he consoles, reassures, and returns to his gentle human form. This is the most emotionally intimate moment in the entire text — and the model caught it.
admiration peaks at Ch9 (0.18) — Raja Vidya Yoga Ch9 = "The Royal Knowledge, the Royal Secret" Krishna reveals his supreme omnipresence: "I am the ritual. I am the sacrifice. I am the father..." The model reads this as admiration — which makes sense. Krishna is admiring his own cosmic nature as he describes it. This is devotional self-revelation.
joy peaks at Ch4 (0.11) — Jnana-Karma-Sanyasa Yoga Ch4 is where Krishna reveals the ancient lineage of yoga: "I taught this to Vivaswan, who taught Manu, who taught Ikshvaku..." Then: "You are My dear devotee and friend — so I reveal this to you" The joy is relational — the intimacy of teacher choosing student across millennia.
The pipeline — from speaker filter to heatmap
# Step 1: Filter by SPEAKER column — not by name mention in text a_krishna = a_english[a_english['Speaker'] == 'Krishna'].copy() # Step 2: Run GoEmotions on Krishna's 564 verses results = emotion_classifier(a_krishna['Enlgish Translation'].tolist()) # Step 3: Convert to 28-column dataframe — one row per verse emotion_rows = [{d['label']: d['score'] for d in verse} for verse in results] emotion_df = pd.DataFrame(emotion_rows) # Step 4: Average by chapter — collapse 564 rows to 18 chapters chapter_emotions = emotion_df.groupby('Chapter').mean() # Step 5: Remove neutral — it drowns everything else top = chapter_emotions.mean().nlargest(10).index top_filtered = [e for e in top if e != 'neutral']
◆ Krishna's emotional fingerprint
Top emotions: approval, admiration, caring, joy, love, optimism. This is the vocabulary of a teacher who is deeply invested in the student's wellbeing — not a detached philosopher. Even in the most abstract philosophical chapters, the emotional baseline is positive and relational.

Section 8 · Topic Modeling

The Hidden Themes — LDA on 18 Chapters

We grouped all verses by chapter, cleaned the text, and ran LDA to discover 7 recurring philosophical themes — without telling the algorithm what to look for.

The 5-step pipeline
Step 1 — Group all verses by chapter (18 documents) chapters = a2.groupby('Chapter')['Enlgish Translation'] .agg(lambda x: ' '.join(x)) Step 2 — Clean: lowercase → remove punctuation → stopwords → lemmatize cleaned_docs = [cleaning(text) for text in chapters] Step 3 — Build dictionary and corpus dictionary = corpora.Dictionary(cleaned_docs) dictionary.filter_extremes(no_below=2, no_above=0.85) corpus = [dictionary.doc2bow(doc) for doc in cleaned_docs] Step 4 — Find optimal K using coherence score Step 5 — Train LDA with K=7, name the topics

! Why we chose chapter-level not verse-level LDA
Running LDA on individual verses gives 700 tiny documents — most too short for meaningful topic signals. Grouping by chapter gives 18 richer documents where co-occurrences can be detected reliably. The tradeoff: we lose verse-level granularity but gain chapter-level clarity.
Section 9 · Readability

How Complex Is the Language — Chapter by Chapter

We used the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level formula on every verse and averaged by chapter. The result reveals which chapters demand the most from a reader — and why.

How Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level works
# Formula Grade = 0.39 × (words/sentences) + 11.8 × (syllables/words) - 15.59 # In plain terms: Higher score = longer sentences + longer words = harder to read Lower score = shorter sentences + shorter words = easier to read Grade 8 → 8th grade reading level Grade 12 → High school senior level Grade 16 → College level

What spikes and dips mean
Chapter 11 — Vishwaroop (cosmic vision) Longest, most complex sentences in the text Arjuna describes an infinite, terrifying form — language itself strains under the weight Chapter 13 — Kshetra and Kshetrajna (field and knower) Dense philosophical taxonomy — body, soul, nature, spirit Technical vocabulary compressed into short verses
Chapter 1 — Arjuna's Vishada (the battlefield) Narrative prose — names, events, descriptions The most accessible chapter — this is story, not philosophy Chapter 17 — Three kinds of faith (food, sacrifice, gifts) Concrete categories, practical examples Krishna classifying observable behaviors — simpler language
◐ The pattern this reveals
Chapters with concrete narrative or practical classification tend to score lower (easier). Chapters with abstract philosophical taxonomy — soul, field, knower, the unmanifest — score higher. The Gita's difficulty is not uniform. It builds in waves, peaks at the cosmic vision, then descends as it returns to practical instruction in Chapter 18.
